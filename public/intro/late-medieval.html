Late Medieval Period  ·  Novice Deck

The Church Builds in Stone

c. 900 – 1400 C.E.

By the year 900, Europe had stabilized enough that bishops and abbots could think big. They tore down the old timber churches and built in stone — first in the rounded, heavy style we call Romanesque, and then, starting around 1140, in the soaring, light-filled style we call Gothic.

The buildings themselves were the great artistic achievement of this period. Masons learned to push walls higher and windows wider without the structure collapsing. Flying buttresses carried the weight outward. Pointed arches directed it downward. The result was walls thin enough to fill with stained glass.

Painting and sculpture served the buildings. Carved figures stood in the arched frames above church doors. Painted panels stood on altars. Illustrated manuscripts traveled from abbey to abbey. Everything was made for a religious setting, and religious meaning still governed every artistic choice.

As you study each work, notice where it would have been placed in a church — over the door, on the altar, in a chapel, on a pillar. The location tells you who was meant to see it and what they were supposed to feel.

Toward the end of this period, something shifts. Painters like Cimabue and then Giotto begin to show figures with weight, gesture, and emotion. The flat, symbolic style of the earlier Middle Ages starts to give way. The Renaissance is coming.
