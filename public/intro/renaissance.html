Renaissance Period  ·  Novice Deck

The Church Rediscovers Rome

c. 1400 – 1600 C.E.

Renaissance means “rebirth.” What was reborn was the art and thought of ancient Greece and Rome, which medieval artists had largely set aside. Starting in the city-states of Italy — Florence above all — painters and sculptors looked again at ancient statues and ruins and asked: how did those artists make figures that look so real?

They worked out the answers systematically. Brunelleschi figured out geometric perspective — how to show depth on a flat surface. Alberti wrote it down. Leonardo dissected corpses to understand how the body works beneath the skin. Michelangelo studied ancient sculpture obsessively. The result was art that showed the visible world more accurately than any European artist had managed since Rome.

Almost all of this work was still paid for by the Church — by popes, bishops, and wealthy families buying their way into heaven with chapels and altarpieces. But the subjects began to change. Classical myths returned. Portraits became more important. The human figure, in all its physical beauty, moved to the center of the picture.

A note on geography: the Renaissance began Catholic and stayed that way in Italy and Spain. In northern Europe — Germany, the Netherlands, England — the Protestant Reformation (starting 1517) pushed back against the elaborate church art the Renaissance produced. As you study these works, notice whether the artist seems to be showing off skill, inviting prayer, or doing both at once.
