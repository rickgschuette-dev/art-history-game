Renaissance Period  ·  Novice Deck

The Church Rediscovers Rome

c. 1400 – 1600 C.E.

Renaissance means “rebirth.” Artists and thinkers renewed their interest in the art and ideas of ancient Greece and Rome, which medieval cultures had also studied and adapted. In the city-states of Italy — Florence above all — painters and sculptors looked closely at ancient statues and ruins and asked: how did those artists make figures that look so real?

They explored these questions in different ways. Brunelleschi investigated geometric perspective — a way to suggest depth on a flat surface — and Alberti wrote about it. Leonardo studied anatomy, and Michelangelo studied ancient sculpture. Their work contributed to new approaches to representing bodies and space.

Church leaders, religious communities, civic groups, rulers, and wealthy families commissioned art for churches, public spaces, and homes. Religious subjects remained important, while classical myths, portraits, and the study of the human figure also occupied artists and their patrons.

Religious change and art: Beginning in 1517, the Protestant Reformation challenged the authority and teachings of the Roman Catholic Church. In some Protestant communities in northern Europe, religious images were removed from churches or destroyed; attitudes toward images varied among Protestants. Meanwhile, Catholic patrons in Italy and other regions continued to commission churches and religious art to teach and inspire devotion. The Catholic Reformation, including the response often called the Counter-Reformation, brought efforts to renew the Church and respond to Protestant challenges. At the Council of Trent (1545–1563), Church leaders reaffirmed the value of sacred images while calling for their proper use. The later cases in this deck explore how churches and artworks served devotion—and why people sometimes disagreed about what belonged in a religious picture.

Sources: The Council of Trent and the call to reform art; The early modern era: the 16th century; The Northern Renaissance Gallery.
