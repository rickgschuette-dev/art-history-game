The Church Paid for the Art

c. 300 – 900 C.E.

After the Roman Empire fell apart, the Christian Church became the most powerful institution in Europe. Bishops and abbots had money; most everyone else did not. So the Church paid for almost all the art made during these centuries.

That shaped what artists made and how they made it. A painting or mosaic had a job to do: teach the Gospel story to people who could not read, fill a church with beauty that pointed toward heaven, or remind worshipers of the saints they were praying to. Looking like real life was not the point. Conveying meaning was.

Artists used gold to stand for divine light. They made holy figures large and frontal, staring directly at the viewer. They flattened space and simplified bodies. These were not mistakes — they were choices.

As you study each work, ask: What was this made for? An altar? A manuscript? A mosaic on a church wall? The purpose usually explains the style.

You will also notice that works from this period often look similar across very different places — Spain, Ireland, Italy, Egypt. The Church provided both the money and the visual vocabulary that artists shared across a wide world.
