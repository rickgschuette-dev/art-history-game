Winged Victory of Samothrace

Artist unknown · circa 200–190 B.C.E. · Louvre, Paris

Look closely at the wind and motion

Magnifier

Detail · repaired wing

The two wings seen from below. The nearer wing has been pieced together from ancient fragments with modern steel armature visible inside; the farther wing preserves more of its ancient feather carving.

Nike, Greek for “victory,” was the winged goddess of triumph. Greek cities set up images of her to celebrate military victories. This monument commemorates a naval victory: the goddess stands on the prow of a warship as if she has just alighted to signal the win.

The statue was discovered in 1863 on the Aegean island of Samothrace, in fragments scattered across a hillside sanctuary. The head and arms have never been found. What survives is the torso, the wings, one reconstructed from fragments, and the great curved drapery blown back against the body by the sea wind.

This is the Hellenistic phase of Greek sculpture, two centuries after the calm balance of Polykleitos. The Classical ideal body is still there, but the Classical calm is gone. A Classical Nike would have stood still. This one is caught as her feet touch the prow: wings still spread for landing, drapery pressed by the wind of flight, and the whole figure leaning into the next step.

Words to know

Hellenistic

The last and most dramatic phase of ancient Greek art, from about 323 B.C.E. to about 30 B.C.E. Hellenistic sculptors kept the Classical ideal body but broke the Classical calm, carving figures in motion, pain, ecstasy, and dramatic moments.

Wet drapery

A sculptor’s technique that makes carved cloth look soaked and clinging to the body beneath. The folds pull tight across the chest and legs, leaving anatomy visible under the fabric. It began in the late Classical period and became more extreme in Hellenistic sculpture.

Votive offering

An object dedicated to a god as a gift of thanks for help received. A city that won a naval battle might dedicate a marble Nike; a farmer might dedicate a small bronze bull. This Winged Victory was a large civic votive for a naval victory.

What to notice
  • The figure is caught mid-landing. Both wings are still outstretched behind her, and the drapery streams back as if blown by the wind of her own flight. Her left foot has just touched the prow below.
  • The drapery is “wet.” Cloth clings tightly enough that the stomach, navel, and shape of a leg are visible beneath it. Folds across the chest pull in long parallel lines toward the hips.
  • The statue stands on the carved prow of a marble warship. Its original hillside setting on Samothrace probably had the sea visible below, making it seem as if Nike had just landed on the ship.
  • The head, arms, and parts of the wings are missing. The statue was discovered in fragments and reassembled; most surviving marble is ancient, while part of one wing is reconstruction.
  • In the detail, the nearer wing is pieced together with visible modern steel armature. The farther wing is better preserved and shows the ancient feather carving.

Why this matters later

Hellenistic drama—with motion, cloth blown by wind, and figures caught mid-action—becomes a direct ancestor of Baroque art. In the Baroque deck, you will meet Bernini’s Ecstasy of St. Teresa, which uses carved cloth and divine wind for a related dramatic effect. The Winged Victory turns marble into a body moving through air.
