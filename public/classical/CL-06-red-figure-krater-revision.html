Red-figure krater

Euphronios, painter · circa 515 B.C.E. · Attica

Close view of the upper bands: Greek key meander, lotus-and-palmette frieze, and a komos procession of figures with lyres, flutes, and wine vessels: a scene fitting for a wine-mixing vessel.

Greek vases are not just utensils. They are the only large-scale Greek paintings that survive in quantity. Painted sculpture has worn away and frescoed walls have crumbled, but vases buried as grave goods or thrown into trash pits have survived by the thousands. Every Greek painter whose name we know, we know from vase signatures.

This krater was painted by Euphronios around 515 B.C.E., when Athenian painters invented the red-figure technique. In the earlier black-figure method, painters drew figures in black silhouette on red clay and cut the interior details with a needle. Red-figure reversed that: figures were left in the clay’s natural red-orange, and the background was painted glossy black around them. Interior details were then drawn with a brush.

The main scene is Herakles battling the Amazons, one of his Twelve Labors. The upper band shows a komos, a festive procession returning from a drinking party, with lyres, flutes, and wine jars. For a vase used to mix wine at a men’s symposium, both scenes were appropriate.

Words to know

Red-figure

A Greek vase-painting technique invented in Athens about 530 B.C.E. Figures are left in the orange-red color of the clay against a glossy black painted background, and interior details are drawn with a brush. The earlier black-figure technique did the opposite.

Krater (KRAY-ter)

A large, wide-mouthed Greek bowl for mixing wine with water. It was standard equipment at the men’s drinking party called the symposium; smaller cups were dipped into the krater to be filled.

Mythology

The old stories of Greek and Roman gods, heroes, and monsters. Greek vase painters, like later European painters, used mythology as one of their main subjects: Herakles, Dionysos, Zeus, and more.

What to notice
  • The figures are orange-red against a glossy black background. This is red-figure: figures are reserved in the clay’s natural color, the background is painted black around them, and interior lines are drawn with a brush.
  • The vase is divided into horizontal bands: a Greek key meander, a lotus-and-palmette frieze, a narrow figurative band of komos figures, a dentil strip, and Herakles battling the Amazons on the belly.
  • In the main scene, armored warriors carry round shields and long spears. The battle is told entirely in silhouette and brushed line, without color, shading, or perspective.
  • The komos band, seen in the detail, shows men returning from a drinking party with lyres, flutes, and wine vessels. The subject fits the vase’s own function.
  • Every curve, muscle outline, and drapery fold is drawn with a confident brush line. This freedom to draw rather than scratch is what makes red-figure a breakthrough.

Why this matters later

Greek vase painting is where the Western tradition of drawing from mythology begins. In the Renaissance deck, you will meet Botticelli’s Primavera and The Birth of Venus, large paintings of classical gods made nearly two thousand years later. They are among the first major Renaissance works to take their subjects from Greek and Roman myth rather than the Bible. That long story begins on painted vases like this one.
